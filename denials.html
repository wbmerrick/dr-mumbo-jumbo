The Denial Playbook

They said no. Here's how to make them look again.

Roughly 1 in 5 in-network claims is denied, and fewer than 1 in 100 denials is ever appealed, so insurers almost never have to defend the no. When people do push back, a large share of denials are reversed. This is the playbook, move by move.

1

Don't pay. Don't panic. Decode.

A denial is the opening position, not the verdict, and the bill attached to it isn't due the day it arrives. Your first job is to understand exactly why they said no, in their own paperwork:

  • Find the denial reason code on the EOB or letter (CO-50, CO-197, PR-204…) and look it up in the code library. Each one has a different winning counter.
  • Or paste the whole letter into the free translator and get it back in plain English.
  • Note the exact words: "not medically necessary," "no prior authorization," "out of network," "not a covered benefit." The appeal you write depends on which one they used.
2

Make one phone call, on the record

Some denials are coding slips or missing-paperwork problems that die on a single call. Call the member number on your card:

You, to the insurer"I'm calling about claim [number], denied on [date] with reason [code]. Can you tell me exactly what was missing or why it didn't meet criteria? What specific clinical criteria did the reviewer use? And can you send me the documents the decision relied on? If this just needs a corrected code or records from my doctor, what exactly should they send?"
  • Write down the date, the rep's name and a reference number for the call. Every time.
  • Ask whether the provider can simply resubmit a corrected claim. Many "denials" are really clerical rejections.
3

Collect your evidence

  • The itemized bill from the provider: not the summary, the line-by-line version with codes.
  • Your medical records for the service: visit notes, test results, orders. You're entitled to copies.
  • The insurer's criteria: the clinical policy or guideline they used to deny. Ask for it by name; plans generally must share the basis for a denial on request.
  • A letter of medical necessity from the ordering clinician: short, specific, and the single strongest thing in most appeal files. Offices rarely have time to write one from scratch, so hand them a draft. The Fight Pack writes it in your doctor's voice, ready to edit and sign.
4

File the internal appeal, in writing

This is the formal "look again," decided by the plan itself, and it's where most reversals happen. Your letter should answer the denial reason point by point, cite your records, and request the reviewer's criteria and credentials.

Paste your denial into the free generator and it drafts the letter for you: argument matched to the reason code, records request included, brackets where your details go.

  • File well before the deadline on your letter (often 180 days from the denial, but your letter controls).
  • Send it through the member portal or by certified mail; keep copies of everything.
  • If care is urgent, say so. Expedited appeals exist for situations that can't wait, with decisions typically required in about 72 hours.
5

Lost round one? Go external.

After a final internal denial, most plans must offer external review: an independent reviewer, not the insurer, decides, and the decision generally binds the plan. This is the step almost nobody takes, and it exists precisely because insurers grading their own homework needed a referee.

  • Your final denial letter must explain how to request it and the deadline (commonly about four months under the federal process; state processes vary).
  • Medical-necessity denials are exactly what external review is built for.
  • Medicare, Medicaid and employer self-funded plans each have their own version of this ladder; the denial letter names yours.
6

Escalate sideways while you wait

  • State insurance department: a complaint is free, gets a case number, and insurers answer to these regulators. Search "[your state] department of insurance complaint."
  • Hospital financial assistance: for the bill itself, nonprofit hospitals must have charity-care policies. Ask for the application in writing, whatever your income.
  • Surprise out-of-network bills: emergency care, or out-of-network providers at an in-network facility, are exactly what the No Surprises Act protects against. Say those words to the billing office.
  • Your HR benefits team (employer plans): they chose the insurer, and a nudge from the client moves faster than a nudge from a member.
