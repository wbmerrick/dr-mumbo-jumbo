Denial Playbook

They said nobody got prior authorization.

Usually CO-197. The service needed advance approval and the claim shows none. For in-network care that is typically the office’s duty rather than your bill — and even when it isn’t, the care still has to be judged on its merits.

1

Find out whose job the authorization was

For in-network care, getting prior authorization is normally the provider’s responsibility, and most network contracts bar the office from billing you when they miss it. Before you argue with the insurer, call the billing office.

You, to the billing office"The claim came back CO-197, no prior authorization. Were you required to obtain the authorization under your contract with this plan? If so, can you request retro-authorization, or write the balance off? I’d like that answer in writing."
  • If they were contracted to get it and didn’t, this may never be your bill at all.
  • For out-of-network care you arranged yourself, the duty is more likely yours — but keep reading, because the service still has to be judged on its merits.
2

Ask for retroactive authorization

Many plans will authorize after the fact when the care was appropriate and the paperwork simply didn’t go in. There is usually a time limit, so this call happens this week, not next month.

  • The office submits the clinical records that would have supported the request at the time.
  • Emergency care generally cannot be made to wait for prior authorization — if the service was emergent, say so plainly and in writing.
  • Urgent ongoing care can qualify for an expedited decision, in about 72 hours.
3

Appeal on both grounds at once

A no-authorization denial has two separate arguments inside it, and a good appeal makes both:

  • Procedural: authorization was obtained, or was not required for this service, or was the contracted provider’s duty — attach the reference number, the contract language, or the plan’s own authorization list.
  • Clinical: the care was medically necessary and would have been approved had it been requested. Attach the records and a short letter from the ordering clinician.

Making only the procedural argument lets the plan deny again on necessity. Make both the first time.

4

Know where this one sits with external review

Independent external review is built for denials that turn on medical judgment. A pure paperwork dispute — "no auth on file" and nothing else — may be treated as contractual rather than clinical, and fall outside it.

  • That is exactly why the clinical argument in move 3 matters: it keeps a medical-judgment question alive in the file.
  • Read the eligibility language on your final denial letter before assuming either way.
  • If external review is closed to you, the state insurance department complaint and, on an employer plan, your HR benefits team are the next levers.
5

Do not pay while any of this is open

A CO-prefixed code marks the amount as a contractual obligation — between the plan and the provider, not a patient balance. If a bill for it still lands in your mailbox, that is worth a question before it is worth a payment.

  • Ask the office, in writing, to identify the contract term that makes it yours.
  • Keep every reference number. Paying does not pause the appeal clock, and it rarely gets refunded quickly.
