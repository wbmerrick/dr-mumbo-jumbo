Denial Playbook

They said it isn’t a covered benefit.

Printed as CO-204, PR-204 or CO-96. This is the hardest category to overturn, and the one where knowing the rules saves you the most wasted effort — including knowing when to stop appealing and start negotiating.

1

Make them point at the words

"Not covered" is a claim about your plan document, so ask for the sentence.

You, to the insurer"Claim [number] was denied as not a covered benefit. Please identify the exact exclusion in my plan document by section and page, and send me that page. I’d also like the remark codes that explain the decision."
  • Then read it yourself in your Summary Plan Description or evidence of coverage.
  • Exclusions are often narrower than the denial implies, and they frequently carry exceptions the reviewer skipped.
2

Check whether it is really a coding problem

A large share of "not covered" denials are miscodes wearing a policy’s clothes.

  • The classic is a preventive service billed with a diagnostic code, which flips a no-cost screening into an uncovered charge.
  • Compare the codes on the itemized bill against what actually happened in the room.
  • If they don’t match, this is a corrected claim from the provider, not an appeal from you. That path is faster.
3

Look for the medical-judgment angle

Be clear-eyed here, because this is the category where people waste the most time: a denial that turns only on contract language or eligibility, with no clinical question in it, generally falls outside federal external review. The independent reviewer is there to second-guess medical judgment, not to rewrite your policy.

  • So the question that decides your odds is whether a clinical question exists: is the service excluded outright, or excluded unless criteria are met?
  • "Covered only when medically necessary," "covered for certain diagnoses," "non-experimental" — any of those puts medical judgment back in play, and the external door back on the latch.
  • If there is a clinical hook, appeal it as a necessity case and say so explicitly in the letter.
4

Ask for an exception, not just a reversal

Plans have exception machinery that lives outside the appeal ladder, and it is often the better route for a true exclusion.

  • Formulary or benefit exceptions for drugs and devices, where the clinician certifies the alternatives failed or are unsuitable.
  • Network adequacy exceptions, if nobody in network can actually deliver the service in a reasonable time or distance.
  • On an employer plan, the employer sometimes has discretion the call-centre does not. Your HR benefits team can ask.
5

Were you warned beforehand?

If the denial came back as PR-204 — the patient-responsibility version — the question of advance notice becomes the argument.

  • Nobody told you it wasn’t covered before you agreed to it? Say that in writing to the provider.
  • On Medicare, that warning has a specific form, the Advance Beneficiary Notice. No signed ABN is a real position, not a complaint.
6

If the exclusion holds, change the conversation

When the service genuinely is not a benefit, the fight stops being about coverage and starts being about price — and that fight you can still win.

  • Ask the provider for the self-pay or cash price. It is often far below the billed charge.
  • Nonprofit hospitals must maintain financial assistance policies. Ask for the application in writing whatever you earn.
  • Ask for an interest-free payment plan before anything goes to collections.
