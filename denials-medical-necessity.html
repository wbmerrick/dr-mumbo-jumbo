Denial Playbook

They said it wasn’t medically necessary.

Often printed as CO-50. It means a reviewer applied a written policy to your paperwork and decided it fell short. It is not a ruling that you weren’t sick, and it is the denial most likely to be reversed when somebody pushes back.

1

Get the criteria they judged you against

A medical-necessity denial is a reviewer applying a written clinical policy to the paperwork in front of them. You cannot argue with a policy you have not read, so the first move is to make them hand it over.

You, to the insurer"I’m appealing claim [number], denied [date] as not medically necessary. Please send me the specific clinical policy or criteria the reviewer applied, the reviewer’s specialty and credentials, and the complete file the decision relied on."
  • Plans generally must give you the basis for the denial on request, free of charge.
  • Read the criteria line by line. Most appeals are won by showing your chart already meets them, not by arguing the criteria are wrong.
  • Write down the date, the rep’s name, and a reference number.
2

Find the gap between your chart and their checklist

Line the policy up against your records and mark every criterion you already meet, with the date and the document that proves it. What is left is the gap, and the gap is what the appeal has to close.

  • Conservative therapy first? Name the drugs, doses and dates you tried, and what happened.
  • Severity threshold? Point at the score, the measurement, the failed test.
  • If a criterion genuinely does not fit your case, say why your situation is the exception — don’t pretend it fits.
3

The letter of medical necessity decides most of these

The single strongest document in a medical-necessity appeal is a short letter from the clinician who ordered the service. It should state the diagnosis, what was tried before, why this service and not a cheaper one, and what happens without it.

  • Most offices have no spare hour to write one, so hand them a draft and ask them to correct and sign it. The Fight Pack writes it in the treating clinician’s voice.
  • Ask the office whether they will do a peer-to-peer review: a direct call between your doctor and the plan’s reviewer. Some denials die on that call.
4

File the internal appeal in writing, point by point

Answer their stated reason in their own vocabulary. Quote the criterion, then show the record that satisfies it. Attach the clinician letter, the itemized bill and the relevant notes.

  • You generally have 180 days from the denial notice to file — your letter states your deadline, and it controls.
  • Decisions typically run about 30 days for care you have not had yet, about 60 days for care already given.
  • If waiting would harm you, request an expedited appeal: those are decided in about 72 hours.
  • The free generator drafts this letter from your denial.
5

This is exactly what external review was built for

If the plan upholds its own denial, you can usually take it to an independent external reviewer whose decision binds the plan. Federal rules make denials involving medical judgment — medical necessity, appropriateness, level of care, effectiveness — eligible for that review.

  • Your final denial letter must explain how to request it and by when; the federal standard gives you at least four months.
  • Standard decisions land in about 45 days, expedited in about 72 hours.
  • Almost nobody uses this step. That is the whole argument for using it.
Why this one is winnable

A reviewer’s opinion is not a finding of fact

Nobody examined you. A clinician who has never met you read a file against a checklist and decided it fell short. That decision can be re-made by your own doctor’s documentation and, failing that, by an independent reviewer who does not work for the plan. Of all the reasons printed on denial letters, this is the one with the most room to move — and the one people most often pay without arguing.
