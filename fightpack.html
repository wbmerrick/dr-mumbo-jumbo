Your deadline tracker

Dates below are computed from what your letter says (the notice date and the number of days it gives you) and, where the letter is silent, from the general rules. The deadline printed on your denial letter always wins. Adjust the inputs if they're off.

    General rules for employer and marketplace plans come from the federal claims-procedure regulations (29 CFR 2560.503-1: at least 180 days to appeal; decisions within 72 hours urgent, 30 days pre-service, 60 days post-service; 45 CFR 147.136: external review within 4 months, decided within 45 days). Medicare Advantage notices give 65 days to appeal; decisions are due in 30 days (pre-service), 60 days (payment) or 72 hours (expedited), and an upheld denial goes automatically to Medicare's independent reviewer. Original Medicare gives 120 days to ask for a redetermination, generally decided within 60 days; a reconsideration can then be requested within 180 days. Medicaid, TRICARE, and some self-funded plans use other clocks — if your letter says one of those, use the dates it prints. The next level's deadline counts from the date on the decision letter, so the tracker leaves it undated.

    Straight answers

    Will my doctor actually sign it?

    Usually, yes — the hard part was never willingness, it was the 45 minutes nobody has. You hand over a letter that already answers the insurer's reason in their language; the office corrects what's wrong, adds specifics in the brackets, and signs. The kit includes a half-page note for the office explaining exactly that.

    Why not just the one-page appeal letter?

    The one-page appeal letter is a good round one. The Fight Pack adds the document that carries the most weight (the doctor's letter), the scripts that get you the insurer's own criteria, the records request, the round-two external-review filing, and the deadlines — the parts people skip and then lose on.

    Does it work for any denial?

    It reads the denial first and fights it on its own terms: medical-necessity and prior-authorization denials get the doctor's letter of medical necessity; an out-of-network emergency bill gets a clinician's emergency attestation and the surprise-billing argument; a coding or bundling denial gets a corrected-claim request for the billing office, because that is who actually fixes it. Medicare Advantage and Medicaid use different clocks; the kit flags that and uses the deadline on your letter.

    What do you keep?

    Nothing. Your documents go to the AI model once to write the kit and are not stored. The finished kit stays in your browser only — print it or copy it somewhere safe.

    The serious bit: Dr Mumbo Jumbo is an educational self-advocacy tool. The Fight Pack is a set of drafts for you, and your clinician, to review, complete, and send yourselves. It does not provide legal, medical, or financial advice, does not file anything on your behalf, does not create a doctor–patient relationship, and makes no promises about outcomes. Appeal rights and deadlines vary by plan and state; your denial letter and plan documents control. Drafts are generated by AI and can contain mistakes — read every line before sending.
