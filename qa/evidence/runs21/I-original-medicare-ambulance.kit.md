**Type of denial:** Medical necessity — ambulance transport. "Medicare won't pay for this ambulance service because the information provided does not show that other forms of transportation would have endangered your health."

**Plan:** Original Medicare (the notice is a Medicare Summary Notice for Part B from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services); you, for your father — a representative form comes first.

**Urgent:** No — standard review (the ambulance service was already received). An appeal of care already completed is decided on the standard timeline.

**What they're really saying:** Medicare covers ambulance transport only when the patient's medical condition is such that other transportation would endanger their health. The denial says the information sent with the claim did not demonstrate that standard — not that your father did not meet it, but that the documentation did not show it.

**What they have to show:** The notice applies Medicare's ambulance medical-necessity standard: "Medicare covers ambulance services only when you need them for a medical condition and other transportation could endanger your health." Request the ambulance coverage policy (the LCD or NCD for ambulance services) to see the detailed criteria.

**Your strongest argument:** Your father lost consciousness, then awoke gray, sweaty, and with chest pain; the emergency department found his heart rate was very slow and kept him overnight for cardiac monitoring and medication adjustment. A patient with syncope, diaphoresis, chest pain, and bradycardia requiring admission meets the test that other transportation would have endangered his health — he could not safely sit upright in a car, his condition was unstable and required immediate evaluation for a life-threatening cardiac problem, and any delay or attempt at non-emergency transport could have worsened his condition or caused another loss of consciousness. The transport was by the 911 system in response to an emergency call, which supports that it was medically urgent.

**Case strength:** Good — one thing missing. The clinical facts strongly support medical necessity, but Medicare denied the claim because the ambulance service did not submit documentation of those facts with the claim.

**What would make it decisive:**
- The ambulance trip report or run sheet documenting the patient's condition at the scene (loss of consciousness, chest pain, appearance, vital signs including the slow heart rate if measured).
- The emergency-department record from Westbrook Regional Medical Center showing the findings on arrival (bradycardia, the decision to admit, the cardiac monitoring and medication changes).
- A letter from the emergency physician or the admitting physician explaining why other transportation would have endangered the patient's health given these findings.

**Who fixes this:** You file the redetermination by the deadline, and you ask Tri-County Emergency Medical Services and Westbrook Regional Medical Center to send their records to the Medicare claims office — the ambulance report and the hospital emergency-department and admission records — along with a physician letter if possible. The appeal goes in with or without those records by the deadline; getting them strengthens it.

**What you may actually owe:** The notice says "Maximum You May Be Billed $1,398.10" for the two ambulance line items. For Original Medicare with a participating ambulance provider, if the redetermination approves the service, you would owe your Part B coinsurance (typically 20% of the Medicare-approved amount, which is generally much lower than the billed amount). If the ambulance provider is non-participating or does not accept assignment, the amount could be higher. Do not pay the disputed amount while the redetermination is pending; ask the ambulance company to hold the bill pending the appeal.

**Do this first:** Complete the CMS-1696 Appointment of Representative form (or send a signed letter authorizing you to act on your father's behalf) so you can file and track the redetermination for him.
