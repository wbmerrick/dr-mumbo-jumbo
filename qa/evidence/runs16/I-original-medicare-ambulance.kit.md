**Type of denial:** Medical necessity. "Medicare won't pay for this ambulance service because the information provided does not show that other forms of transportation would have endangered your health."

**Plan:** Original Medicare Part B (Medicare Summary Notice from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services). You, Renee Pruitt-Cole, for your father — an Appointment of Representative form (CMS-1696) or power of attorney comes first.

**Urgent:** No — standard review, because the ambulance transport was already received on August 26 and care is complete.

**What they're really saying:** Medicare will pay for ambulance transport only when the patient's medical condition at the time made other transportation dangerous to their health. The ambulance crew's report to Medicare did not document findings that showed why a private car or wheelchair van would have been unsafe.

**What they have to show:** Medicare's ambulance medical-necessity criteria — generally that the patient's condition required ambulance-level care (for example they were bedridden, could not sit upright, needed monitoring or treatment en route, or had an acute emergency condition) and that other transportation would have endangered their health.

**Your strongest argument:** Your father had just lost consciousness and on awakening had chest pain, was gray and sweaty, and was found in the ER to have a very slow heart rate requiring overnight admission and medication adjustment — all red-flag findings of a potentially life-threatening cardiac emergency at the time of transport. He could not sit upright in a car, and your mother does not drive. A private vehicle is not validated for an unstable patient with chest pain and a syncopal episode who may need resuscitation or cardiac monitoring en route.

**Case strength:** Good — one thing missing. The clinical facts support emergency ambulance need, but the ambulance crew's patient-care report likely lacked the specific medical findings Medicare requires (such as vital signs, inability to sit, chest-pain severity, altered mental status).

**What would make it decisive:**
- The ambulance crew's patient-care report from August 26, 2026, showing vital signs, symptoms, and level of consciousness documented at the scene
- The ER record from Westbrook Regional Medical Center from August 26, 2026, documenting the presenting heart rate, chest pain, and admission for bradycardia
- A letter from the ER or admitting physician explaining why other transportation would have endangered the patient's health given the syncopal episode, chest pain, and unstable heart rate

**Who fixes this:** You file the appeal — the authorization form first, then the appeal letter with the ambulance report and ER records by February 11, 2027. Call the ambulance company to request a copy of the patient-care report, and ask Westbrook Regional Medical Center for the ER record.

**What you may actually owe:** The notice shows a maximum of $1,398.10, but for Original Medicare this is the full billed amount — if the appeal succeeds, Medicare pays 80% of the approved amount and your father pays 20% coinsurance (the Part B deductible is already met). If the ambulance company participates in Medicare, they must accept Medicare's decision and may not bill more than the approved coinsurance; if they do not participate, they may bill up to 115% of the Medicare-approved amount. Ask the ambulance company whether they participate in Medicare, and do not pay the disputed amount while the appeal is pending.
