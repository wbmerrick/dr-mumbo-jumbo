Denial Playbook

They stamped it out of network.

Sometimes that is correct and sometimes it is a bill you are not allowed to be sent. Emergency care, and the out-of-network clinician you never chose at an in-network hospital, are precisely what the federal No Surprises Act was written to stop.

1

Work out whether federal protection covers you

The No Surprises Act bans balance billing in three situations. If you are in one of them, this is not a negotiation — it is a rule.

  • Most emergency services, including emergency mental health care, and the care that stabilises you before and after.
  • Non-emergency care from an out-of-network provider at an in-network facility — a hospital, hospital outpatient department, or ambulatory surgical centre. The classic case is the anesthesiologist, radiologist or pathologist you never chose.
  • Out-of-network air ambulance, if your plan covers air ambulance at all.

Two gaps worth knowing before you argue: non-emergency care at an out-of-network facility is not protected, and ground ambulance is not covered by these protections — that bill may genuinely be yours.

2

Check what you were actually charged

Where the protection applies, your cost sharing must be no more than the in-network amount, and it counts toward your in-network deductible and out-of-pocket maximum.

  • Compare the EOB’s patient-responsibility line against what in-network care would have cost you.
  • Any balance beyond that in-network share is the piece that should not exist.
  • The provider and the plan settle the rest between themselves. That dispute is not yours to fund.
3

Did you sign something?

For some non-emergency care at an in-network facility, a provider may ask you to waive these protections in writing. That consent is voluntary, and it has rules.

  • The notice must reach you at least 72 hours before the service. If the appointment was booked less than 72 hours out, it must reach you the day the appointment was made — and for same-day care, at least 3 hours before the service.
  • Consent cannot be taken for emergency services before you are stable, for unforeseen urgent care, or for ancillary services such as anesthesiology, pathology, radiology and neonatology.
  • A form pushed at you on a gurney, or at check-in the morning of, is worth raising with the plan and the regulator.
4

Say the words, in writing, to both sides

Billing offices and plans route these by keyword. Use the statute’s name.

You, to billing and to the plan"This claim is subject to the No Surprises Act. The service was [emergency care / out-of-network care at an in-network facility], so my cost sharing is limited to the in-network amount and I cannot be balance billed. Please reprocess and send me a corrected statement."
  • Send it through the member portal or by certified mail and keep copies.
  • Put the facility’s network status and the date of service in the first sentence.
5

Escalate to the people who enforce it

This one has a regulator with a mandate, which most denial categories do not.

  • The federal No Surprises Help Desk takes complaints about balance billing — the current number and the online complaint form are at cms.gov/nosurprises.
  • Your state insurance department handles state-regulated plans and often moves faster.
  • Compliance with these protections can itself be taken to independent external review.
